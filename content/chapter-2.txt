BRAM: I have built rooms that were lies, and I know the sound a lie makes when you step on it. Canvas gives. A brace knocks hollow. Paint smells like paint no matter what sea you have lettered across it. The parlor inside the pear tree did none of those things. The boards took my weight and complained in the voice of old pine. Salt gritted under my boots. The wallpaper moved. I will say that plainly, because later someone will try to make it prettier: the wallpaper moved, the way water moves when a boat has already gone by.

BRAM: Lina went first, because it was her hour more than mine, and because I had not yet earned the right to lead anyone anywhere. Sera came last and held the book-lantern high, so the light would fall on the room and not on our faces. That was kind. I did not want either of them looking at me when I understood the window.

BRAM: It was a round room, if a room grown in a trunk can be said to have chosen a shape. Roots crossed the ceiling like beams I would have been proud to cut, and ashamed to take credit for. A table stood in the middle, set for three, with china that did not match. One cup had a chip the colour of old milk. One plate wore a pattern of pears so faded it looked remembered rather than printed. Steam rose from a pot that had no business being hot. Three chairs waited. None of them faced the door we had used. All three faced a tall window, and through the window there was a sea.

LINA: Those are not our chairs. Look at the cards.

BRAM: She was right, which did not comfort me. Place cards stood against the cups, written in a hand I later learned was their mother's, though at first I only saw that it was careful and a little tired. Mother. Edwin. Lina. My name was not in the room. I had come in anyway, the way a man walks onto a stage after the scene has started because he can hear a flat about to fall.

BRAM: Sera did not sit. She walked the edge of the room and spoke the way she writes, each thing put down once. Salt on the boards. Heat in the pot. No footprints but ours. A sack under the table. At the word sack, Lina was on her knees. I knelt with her, because kneeling is a useful trade when you are too tall and too late. Under the cloth, wrapped in wet sacking, were the pear slips: six of them, roots pale, still alive, smelling of jar-water and a windowsill. Eleven nights in a magic room and the roots had not dried. I hate a miracle that makes me grateful and angry in the same breath. This was that.

LINA: He carried them this far. He set the table, or the hour did, and then he left the one thing that mattered on the floor. Why would he leave them?

SERA: Because this is not the hour's last room. This is a room that opened around whoever stood in it. Sit, and you will see whose weather it borrowed.

BRAM: I did not want to sit. I sat. The chair nearest the window was not labelled, so I took it, and the sea changed before I had finished deciding that the sea was impossible. The moon stayed. The water stayed. The far shore became a road I knew, drowned to the hedges, with the marker stone sticking up like a tooth. I was twenty. The river had taken the low road in the night. My sister was playing the second daughter in a hall two villages over, and I had the cart, and I did not try the long way round the ridge. I told myself the road would be there in the morning. In the morning the road was still water, and the play was over, and she laughed when she saw me and did not know yet that I had chosen a dry coat over her first bow.

BRAM: The tape at my belt unspooled. It slid across the salt like a creature with a simple mind and stopped when the little window showed eleven again. Not miles. Not inches. Nights. I put my hand on it and it twitched, offended, as if measurement were a promise I kept breaking.

BRAM: Lina sat in the chair with her name. I heard her breath change, so I looked, though I had meant not to. Outside her side of the glass the sea had become a kitchen window streaked with rain. A woman with Lina's mouth and Edwin's stubborn brow was pointing at jars along a sill. I could not hear the words. I did not need to. Lina's hand was flat on the table, and the empty cup beside Edwin's card was warm. I touched the rim when she asked me to. It was warm the way a cup is warm when someone has just stood up to fetch a cloth. He was not in the chair. He had been. Or the hour wanted us to believe the difference was small.

SERA: Do not drink. Do not plant them here. A promise put in the wrong room becomes the room. If these slips go into this floor, you will have a kitchen that is also a drowned road, and it will not consent to be an orchard again. Carry them to the last door.

LINA: Then say what you did to the gate, Bram. You have been holding it in your mouth since the shed.

BRAM: There are sentences you rehearse until they sound like carpentry, and then a young woman looks at you over her mother's place card and the carpentry falls out of them. I told the truth. I had seen Edwin come up the lane with the sack on his shoulder and the determined walk of a man who has waited too long for a brave reason. I had followed him as far as the west row. I had watched the first trunk split, not like lightning, like a joint opening along the grain because something inside had swelled. He went through. I called his name once. The orchard answered by growing a second frame closer to the lane, close enough that I could imagine a schoolchild stepping sideways into somebody else's unfinished year.

BRAM: So I locked the gate. Not to keep him in. The lock was on our side, and a room does not respect iron. I locked it to keep the search on the road. I told the clerk he had likely gone after his sister on the coast. I slept in the shed. I measured the new frames every dawn and wrote the numbers down and hated the page. When Lina's letter came back by the evening cart, the nearest door had learned how to stand ajar. I am not asking to be forgiven in a parlor. I am saying the shape of the wrong thing, so you can see it, the way you would sight along a board before you decide it can still be used.

LINA: You left him in here for eleven nights so the village could keep its errands.

BRAM: Yes.

SERA: And if we stand here accusing the lock, the last door finishes without us. Guilt is accurate. It is not a tool. Pick up the sack.

BRAM: Lina picked it up. I took the heavier side, because that is the only skill of mine that has never needed an apology. The sacking soaked my cuff. Six slips knocked softly against my wrist, alive, absurd, ready. Sera closed no book. She put one hand on the window latch, and the sea, the road, the kitchen, all of it, swung inward like a painted shutter that had been a door all along.

BRAM: Cold water took us to the ankle. For three steps I thought I had lied to myself and walked into my own old failure on purpose. Then the water thinned. It became a shine on stone. The parlor folded behind us with a sound like a cloth being shaken once, and we were in a corridor of glass. Pear branches pressed against the panes from the outside, ordinary and enormous. Along both walls stood doors. Shut doors. Dozens. Different heights, different woods, all of them with the settled look of work that has been done and put away.

SERA: Finished hours. Do not open them. They are not yours, and they are not lonely.

BRAM: At the far end, one door was unlatched. Gold light lay on the flags in a long patient stripe. My tape, traitorous instrument, wound itself up and went still, as if even it understood that the next measurement would not be a number I already knew. Lina shifted the sack against her hip. Her face in that gold light was the face of a person who has stopped spending anger and started spending attention. I was glad to follow it. I have followed worse things, including my own fear, and called the following prudence.

BRAM: Behind us, if I turned, I could still smell salt and tea. Ahead, someone was breathing in the slow way of a man who has been holding a weight with his knees for nights and has made a kind of peace with the shaking. I knew that breathing. I had heard it through a gate I should not have locked. The glass hummed when we walked, a low note, almost a welcome, almost a warning. Sera lifted the lantern. The open book made our three shadows long and separate, and then, as we came nearer the light, the shadows joined on the stone like people who had decided to arrive together after all.

BRAM: I said his name. Not as a confession this time. As a knock. The light at the end of the gallery did not flicker, but the unlatched door moved, the width of a hand, and a voice hoarse with disuse answered from the soil beyond it.

EDWIN: If you have the slips, come through. If you have come to tell me to let go, you can turn around. I am not finished.

LINA: We have them. And I am not turning around.

BRAM: That was the middle of the night, and the middle of the job. Everything before it had been a door learning our names. Everything after it would have to be planting, apology, and the sort of courage that looks, from the outside, like kneeling in dirt and doing the ordinary thing at last.
