Finished, not lost

Sera tells how the hour was planted, and how one young tree kept a seam.

Sera

I had believed, for years, that a precise name was a kind of latch. Say what a thing is, and it will consent to stay where you put it. The Closing Gallery corrected me with great courtesy. The doors on either side were already named. I could feel the names the way you feel heat through a cup: a letter signed, a kettle lifted off the boil, an apology spoken into a coat before the owner turned around and offered the coat instead of the speech. Finished hours. They did not need me. They were shining quietly, the way shut books shine in a library after the lamps are lowered, and they were none of my business.

What needed us was the door at the end, and the man kneeling in the narrow daylight beyond it. I will write him as I saw him, because tenderness is not an excuse for blur. Edwin Voss was thinner than the palm-print he had left on the first frame. His hair was stuck to his forehead with sap and sweat. Both knees of his trousers were earth. His hands were empty, and they were held out anyway, not to be taken, but to keep the door from finding its frame. When a room wants to finish without you, it leans. He was leaning back.

Edwin

You took long enough that I started talking to the pears. They are bad company. They only glow.

Lina

You left the slips in a room that thought it was our kitchen. Bram carried them the rest of the way. I am here. Move your hands and tell me where the soil will take them.

Sera

He looked at his sister as if the sentence were water. Then he looked at Bram, and the look was not kind and not cruel. It was the look of a man checking a joint he already knows is weak, to see whether it will hold this one last load. Bram did not defend himself. He set his side of the sack down as if it were china. That, more than any speech, is why I let him stay in the doorway. Some apologies are a posture. His was.

Bram

I'll hold the door. You two know the promise. I know hinges. If it tries to close, it will have to go through my shoulder, and my shoulder has opinions.

Sera

The place beyond the door was almost insulting in its plainness. I had catalogued tempests and parlors, and the heart of the orchard was a strip of ordinary ground between two pear trees, open to a sky the colour of tin. Frost was thinking about the grass and had not yet committed. The young trees on either side were real trees. No frames. No wallpaper. Just bark, and a patch of turned soil where someone had started a row and stopped when he realised the row would not stay a row unless the hour was finished in the right direction.

Edwin

If I let go, the trees keep the promise and forget who made it. The lane stays safe. I stay a story in which I do not come home. I did not like those terms. So I held the door. I could not hold it and plant at the same time. That is the whole of my clever plan. You can laugh later.

Lina

I am not laughing. Mother said before the frost. The frost is on the grass, not on this soil. We are late. We are not lost. Give me the spade, or give me your hands.

Sera

There was no spade. There were hands, and a flat stone, and Bram's tape-measure used, for once, as the thing it had probably been in some earlier and humbler life: a straight edge. I knelt and did what I have never written down as library work. I took the place cards I had slipped from the parlor table when the sea became a shutter. I had told Lina not to take souvenirs. I had taken three scraps of card because a name, laid in the right ground, is not a souvenir. It is a marker. I showed them to her before I used them. She nodded. Edwin made a sound that might have been our mother's name and might have been only breath.

We planted six slips in a short row, which is the correct number for a promise and an awkward number for a legend. One at the end for the woman who had rooted them in jars and then run out of spring. One where Bram set his palm flat, and he said, so quietly I almost granted him privacy, that this one was for a road he had not walked and a bow he had not seen. One in the centre, which Edwin set himself, both hands finally busy with the living thing instead of the door. The last three we put in as a plain orchard puts trees in: deep enough, straight enough, soil pressed so the root cannot rock. Lina poured a little water from her flask into the empty vials and tipped it, carefully, at each stem. It was not enough water. It was the gesture that told the hour we were not hurrying past the work.

When the last soil was firm, the gallery behind Bram changed its mind. I heard it as a soft collective click, polite as a reading room at closing. The shut doors went dark. Not dead. Satisfied. The glass ceased to be glass and became air with branches in it. The gold stripe on the stone pulled back into ordinary dusk. Edwin's door, the one he had been holding, sighed into bark. His hands hovered where the wood had been, and then they dropped, and he looked surprised to have shoulders again.

Edwin

I thought it would take more. A speech. A bargain. Something with blood in it, the way stories charge you at the end.

Lina

It took the planting. You made it large by waiting. Hours are supposed to be shared, Edwin. If you leave them alone they turn into furniture, and then sensible men lock gates, and librarians start believing they can shelve a person.

Bram

The gate is unlocked. It has been unlocked since you stepped through the first door tonight. I am saying that so nobody thanks me for a key.

Sera

We walked out along the gravel that remembered how to be a path. The fog had lifted enough to show the rows. Trunks were trunks. The splits had closed into seams you would only notice if you knew where to put your eyes, and even those seams were fading, sap sealing what a room no longer needed. The pears were pears. They did not glow. I was vain enough to miss the light for one heartbeat, and then I was a librarian again, which is to say I was glad when a book shut because the reader had finished.

One tree had not returned all the way to the ordinary. It stood a little younger than the row, just off the path, with a vertical seam in the bark like a door that had considered opening and chosen, for tonight, not to. I put my palm near it and did not touch. Some latches should be left for the next person who arrives with work in their hands. Lina understood before I spoke. She took her last empty vial, rinsed it, and set it on a root beneath the seam. Not a sample. A cup. An invitation with no instructions on it except the shape of a useful object.

Lina

If someone needs an unfinished hour, they can find this tree. They had better bring the thing they meant to do. I am not coming back to carry anybody's sack but his, and his is planted.

Edwin

I can walk. Stop looking at my knees as if they are a diagnosis. I want the bakery, and I want to sleep in a bed that does not try to become a chapel. And I want to hear, tomorrow, every detail of the sea-room, because I only saw the kitchen, and Bram looks as if the sea was rude to him.

Bram

The sea was accurate. That is ruder.

Sera

At the gate, which stood open on its ordinary iron hinge and did not mean anything mystical at all, I closed the book-lantern. The pages met. The light went out in my hands and remained, faintly, on the wet grass, which was only frost deciding to begin. It settled on the path and on the weeds and on the hem of my dress. It did not touch the new slips. I checked twice, because wonder makes a person sloppy, and sloppy notes are how rooms begin.

Edwin leaned on Lina for exactly ten steps and then refused, out of pride, and then leaned again, out of sense. Bram walked ahead and tested nothing, which for him was a kind of prayer. I wrote one line while the ink could still see the sky, and then I shut the book on it. Finished, not lost. The village ahead of us showed its small lights: the smithy cooled, a dog asleep, a window over the bakery where a clerk had left a lamp because he hoped three lines of bad news might become a person in a doorway. We were four shadows on a lane that had been spared a door. Behind us the young tree kept its seam and did not call us back.

If you ask me what the orchard is now, I can answer without hiding inside a prettier sentence. It is an orchard. It grows pears. It also keeps, in one trunk, a place for an hour that someone is ready to finish rather than display. I will not catalogue that seam unless it opens. Some knowledge is a door, and I have spent tonight learning when to take my hand off the frame and let the people who own the promise do the planting. That was the end of the work. The frost went on with its own small, honest labour, and we went home.
