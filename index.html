Part one · Beginning

The door in the west row

Lina comes home to Low Merrow and finds the pear trees growing rooms.

Lina

The evening cart stopped where the lane gives up being a road and becomes two ruts and a strip of grass. I stepped down with my survey satchel knocking against my hip, and Low Merrow smelled the way it always had after rain: wet leaves, cold iron from the smithy, and pears that had been left too long in the grass. Eleven nights is not a long time to be away from a brother. It is a very long time for a brother to be inside a tree.

I had come from the coast, where I measure orchards that behave like orchards. I write down girth and blight and the angle of a graft. I put sap in glass vials and I do not, as a rule, argue with stories. The letter from the parish clerk had not been a story. It had been three lines. Edwin Voss walked into the west orchard with a sack. The gate was found shut. He did not come to the bakery on the third morning, or the sixth, or the tenth.

Bram

Don't go in until I've said the part you'll hate.

Lina

Bram was at the toolshed when I reached the trees. He is taller than the door he was closing, and his beard is braided with the same twine he uses on young trunks. Wood shavings clung to his forearms. He held a brass measuring tape as if it might apologise. He did not say welcome home. He said that sentence, and then he looked past me at the first row, where the bark had split in clean vertical lines, pale as fresh-cut wood. In three of the trunks the split had become a frame. In one of them a door stood ajar, and the light inside it was the colour of a kitchen lamp. Salt glittered on the threshold. Pears hung on either side of the frame, and two of them were glowing, softly, the way a window glows when someone is still awake.

Bram

They started the night he went in. I want to fell the row before a door opens onto the lane. Wood I understand. A room that grows a window onto somebody's old weather, I don't. If we cut them, the hour ends. He comes out.

Lina

Out forgetting what, I asked him. Bram looked at the tape. The tongue of it had crept forward on its own, and the number in the little window was eleven, and then it was not a number at all. He said that was the part I would hate. Sera said a cut hour does not spill. It seals. Edwin would walk back to the bakery and he would not remember the sack, or the frost, or why his hands were dirty. The village would keep its lane. My brother would keep his name and lose the reason he used it.

I put my hand on the door. The wood was warm, which a living trunk can be, and also not. I took a vial from the satchel, worked the cork free, and caught a thread of sap from the split. It smelled like a kitchen after someone has cried and then done the decent thing and washed the cups. I labelled it, because my hands know how to do that when the rest of me does not. E. Voss. West row. Night eleven.

We argued in the practical way of people who have already decided and are hoping the other person will say it first. Bram talked about the lane, the schoolchildren, the baker's cart, the risk of a door opening behind the cistern. I talked about a promise our mother had made us make in the spring, when she could still sit up and point at the slips she had rooted in jars along the sill. Plant them before the first frost, she had said, even if I am not here to see the fruit. Edwin had waited because the coastal work kept me, and because grief makes a person superstitious about dates. He had gone in alone so the date would not win.

The fog thickened between the rows until the far trees were only seams. That was when Sera Quill came down the path with a lantern made from a book. The pages were open and the light came from between them, and her dress was dark enough that the small stitched stars on it looked like a sky that had been folded up for travel. She looked at my vial, at Bram's tape, and at the door, in that order, which told me she had a method and that I was already inside it.

Sera

You must be Lina. I have three open rooms and one man who will not let the last of them finish itself. I did not write his name in the book until you were here to hear it said properly.

Lina

I said it for her. Edwin. She inclined her head, as if the name had weight and she preferred to carry it carefully. Then she told us what the orchard had been doing while the village argued about whether my brother had run away to the coast, or to a worse idea.

Sera

A room here is an hour someone did not finish. Not a poem. An hour. A decision still wearing its coat. If you burn it, the hour is lost. If you finish the thing the hour came to do, the room remembers how to be a tree. Your brother is not a metaphor trapped in bark. He is a man kneeling in an unfinished act, and the orchard is furnishing his delay.

Bram

Three places, then. Say them so I can hate them accurately.

Sera

This threshold, which is only a door and already too proud of itself. A parlor beyond it that thinks it is a sea, because someone in this village once failed to cross water. And a gallery of shut doors at the orchard's heart, where finished hours go to stop interrupting people. One door there is still unlatched. He is holding it. If it closes without the slips in the ground, the promise stays a room, and rooms spread. You have seen the frames. You can count.

Lina

I could. I counted three frames close by, and a softness farther in that might have been more. The glowing pears pulsed, not brightly, like coals under ash. I thought of my mother's jars on the sill, the roots white and determined, the way Edwin had wrapped them in wet sacking the morning I left for the coast. I had told him I would be back before the frost. I had been wrong by eleven nights and one letter.

I said I was going in. Bram nodded as if I had announced the weather. He looped the tape at his belt. The brass reel clicked, a small honest sound. He said he would come as far as the wood would have him, and that if he told me to step back it would be because the grain was wrong, not because he was brave. I believed the second half. I was not yet ready to ask what he had done with the gate.

Sera

Then we do this as a job. We find the hour. We do the thing it is waiting for. We do not take souvenirs, Lina, not even a beautiful one. Your vials can come. The sap is a note, not a trophy.

Lina

I put the labelled vial back in its leather loop. I was angry, and I was late, and I was more frightened than I intended to show a librarian and a man who measured his fear in inches. The door moved when I touched it, a fraction, as doors do when someone on the other side has just taken their hand away. There was no one on the other side that I could see. There was a smell of salt and tea, and a floor that had no business being a floor.

Behind me, Low Merrow went on being a village. A dog barked once. The smithy's iron ticked as it cooled. Ahead, the light was warm enough to mistake for welcome. I stepped over the salt. Bram ducked under the frame, shavings still on his sleeves, and Sera followed with the stars on her dress catching the lamps of the fruit. The orchard took our weight the way a house takes the weight of people who have finally come home at the wrong time, which is still, I was about to learn, a kind of coming home.

The door did not shut. That was the first kindness. The path behind us remained a path, gravel and fog and the ordinary dark. The second kindness was smaller. On the inside of the frame, at the height of a man's shoulder, there was a smear of soil in the shape of a palm, and in the soil a thread of wet sacking. Edwin had touched here. He had been carrying the slips. He had gone farther in.

I set my own palm over the print. It was smaller. It fit inside his with room to spare, the way it had when we were children and he taught me to hold a graft without crushing the join. I told the wood, and him, and the hour that had sat down in our mother's promise, that we were coming. Then the light deepened, and the floor changed under my boots from bark to boards, and the night's real work began.
